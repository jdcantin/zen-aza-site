Blog — Abbot'S Essays

Blog Introduction Copy

The Abbot's blog is a continuing series of essays and reflections by Richard Reishin Collins on Zen practice, poetry, literature, and life at Stone Nest Dojo in Sewanee, Tennessee. Posts range from dharma commentary to literary criticism to personal memoir, always rooted in decades of practice in the lineage of Kodo Sawaki and Taisen Deshimaru.

Teaching Theme Summary — For Blog Landing Page

1. The Stink of Zen and the Return to Nature

Perhaps the most persistent theme in Collins's essays is the tension between institutional religion and direct experience. In "Stink of Zen, Poison Dragons" (Tricycle, Summer 2026), he distinguishes between the fragrance-accumulating temple and the source one finds in nature. The "stink of Zen" is the accumulation of ritual and habit that can obscure the living practice beneath. True practice requires periodically leaving the temple to return to one's own nature. "The ritual and forms of the temple are fine, but zazen is the key. Zazen does not need a formal ritual space but only a space at the source of one's own nature." His own poem "In the Posture of the Water Dragon" enacts this: rather than taming poison dragons through meditation, he simply takes his own posture — that of the Water Dragon of the Chinese zodiac, born 1952 — and becomes himself.

2. Delusion, Dreams, and the Present Moment

Collins returns repeatedly to Deshimaru's teaching that "Delusion itself is satori." In his New Year's essay (December 2025), he reflects on the joya no kane ceremony: the bell rings 108 times to dispel the 108 bonnos, yet the Four Great Vows remind us, "Delusions are endless; we vow to drop them all." The vow is not to eliminate delusion but to keep turning toward clarity — moment by moment, year by year. His essay on dreams (March 2026) extends this further: even dreams, which Deshimaru regarded as "delusion piled upon delusion," can be revelatory. A vision of carrying his deceased teacher Robert Livingston, who is light "as a feather, light as a father, light as air," becomes an occasion for reflection on impermanence and the continuation of the dharma.

3. The Teacher and the Transmission

Robert Livingston Roshi appears throughout Collins's writing as a presiding spirit — mourned, dreamed of, carried. The poem "The Dead Master" uses the image of the cicada's abandoned exoskeleton: the temple as shell, the teacher's body as shell, the true inheritance being the living practice that takes on new forms. Collins's own relationship to Livingston — as disciple who left for five years (2005–2010), returned, and was asked to take ordination and eventually receive transmission — animates much of his writing on authority, autonomy, and what it means to inherit a practice.

4. Poetry and the Ancient Chinese Poets

Collins's literary background gives his writing unusual density and range. His Stone Nest collection (Shanti Arts, 2025) places him explicitly in conversation with Wang Wei, Han Shan, and others who chose mountain solitude as their practice ground. His translations are not scholarly but responsive — "after" poems that engage the originals across distances of time, culture, and personal awareness. The mountain hermitage at Sewanee becomes his own version of the Chinese poet's retreat: "Here on the mountain we may not be closer to God / but we're closer to the ancient Chinese poets / who chose to be closer to nature and themselves."

5. Community Practice and the Sitting Life

Alongside the more literary essays, the blog documents the rhythm of collective practice: seasonal zazenkai, retreat announcements, and reflections on why sitting together differs from solitary practice. The Spring Zazenkai at NOZT (March 7–8, 2026) is announced with a flyer and accompanied by a dharma reflection. These posts serve as both calendar and teaching.
